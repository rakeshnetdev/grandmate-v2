## Italian Game (C50-C54)
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 develops the light-squared bishop toward f7, the most exposed
point in Black's early position. The Giuoco Piano (3...Bc5) leads to careful,
principled development for both sides, while sharper tries like the Fried Liver
Attack sacrifice material to expose Black's king before it can castle. A classical,
theory-light choice that still rewards accurate calculation once the position opens.

## Ruy Lopez / Spanish Opening (C60-C99)
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 pressures the knight defending e5 rather than attacking f7
directly, and is one of the oldest, most deeply analysed openings in the game. Main
lines (the Closed, Open, Berlin, and Marshall variations among others) can lead to
either long strategic manoeuvring battles or sharp, well-known tactical territory,
depending on Black's third move.

## Scotch Game (C44-C45)
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4 opens the centre immediately rather than developing a piece
first, trading the e- and d-pawns and often leading to open, tactical middlegames
with both sides' pieces active early. Popular as a way to sidestep the deep
theoretical demands of the Ruy Lopez while still fighting directly for the centre.

## Four Knights Game (C46-C49)
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nc3 Nf6 is a symmetrical, solid developing sequence with both
sides bringing knights out before committing to a pawn structure. Considered one of
the safest ways to reach a balanced middlegame from 1.e4 e5, at some cost to
ambition for either side.

## Petrov's Defence / Russian Defence (C42-C43)
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nf6 mirrors White's attack on e5 rather than defending it, a solid,
drawish-reputation defence favoured by players wanting to neutralise White's
first-move initiative with minimal risk, at the cost of somewhat reduced winning
chances with the black pieces.

## King's Gambit (C30-C39)
1.e4 e5 2.f4 offers a pawn immediately for rapid development and open lines toward
Black's king, one of the oldest recorded openings and a hallmark of the Romantic era
of chess. Modern engine analysis has shown Black has reliable ways to hold the
extra pawn, so it appears today mostly as a sharp, practical surprise weapon rather
than a main-line choice at the top level.

## Vienna Game (C25-C29)
1.e4 e5 2.Nc3 keeps options flexible, often preparing an eventual f4 push (the
Vienna Gambit) or quiet development, and can transpose into King's Gambit or Four
Knights structures depending on both players' choices.

## Philidor Defence (C41)
1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 supports the e5 pawn solidly but blocks the dark-squared bishop's
natural development square, a passive but resilient choice associated with slow,
patient defensive play rather than active counterplay.

## Sicilian Defence (B20-B99)
1.e4 c5 is statistically the most popular reply to 1.e4 at every serious level of
play, immediately creating an asymmetrical pawn structure that gives Black realistic
winning chances rather than merely equalising. Major branches include the sharp,
deeply theoretical Najdorf and Dragon variations (both reached via 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4
cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 and a further move), the flexible Taimanov and Kan systems,
and White's quieter alternatives to the main open lines such as the Alapin (2.c3)
and Closed Sicilian (2.Nc3).

## French Defence (C00-C19)
1.e4 e6 builds a solid pawn chain with a later ...d5, at the cost of temporarily
restricting the light-squared bishop behind that chain. The Advance (3.e5), Tarrasch
(3.Nd2), and Winawer (3.Nc3 Bb4, pinning the knight) variations each give the
position a distinct character, from locked strategic manoeuvring to sharp,
double-edged tactics.

## Caro-Kann Defence (B10-B19)
1.e4 c6 prepares ...d5 while keeping the light-squared bishop free to develop
outside the pawn chain, unlike the French — a reputation for solidity that makes it
a common choice for players prioritising a sound structure over early activity.
The Advance, Classical, and Exchange variations, along with the sharper
Panov-Botvinnik Attack against it, each lead to distinctly different pawn structures.

## Scandinavian Defence (B01)
1.e4 d5 challenges the centre immediately, and after 2.exd5 Qxd5 Black's queen comes
out early and typically loses a tempo to an attacking knight move, which is the
main strategic cost weighed against the resulting simple, well-understood positions.

## Pirc and Modern Defences (B06-B09)
Both defences (1.e4 d6/g6, developing ...Nf6, ...g6, and a fianchettoed bishop on
g7) deliberately allow White a large pawn centre with the plan of undermining it
later with moves like ...c5 or ...e5 — a hypermodern approach that trades immediate
central space for flexible, delayed counterplay.

## Alekhine's Defence (B02-B05)
1.e4 Nf6 provokes White's central pawns to advance by attacking e4 directly, then
aims to undermine that advanced pawn chain later with moves such as ...d6 and ...c5 —
a hypermodern strategy that accepts a temporary space disadvantage for long-term
targets to attack.

## Queen's Gambit (D06-D69)
1.d4 d5 2.c4 offers a flank pawn (not really a true gambit, since White typically
recovers it) to gain central influence and open lines. The Queen's Gambit Accepted
(2...dxc4) surrenders the centre briefly for active piece play, while the Declined
(2...e6) and the related Slav (2...c6) build a solid classical centre for Black
instead.

## Slav and Semi-Slav Defences (D10-D19, D43-D49)
1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6 supports the d5 pawn while keeping the light-squared bishop free,
unlike the Queen's Gambit Declined proper. The Semi-Slav (adding ...Nf6, ...e6, and
...Nc3 for both sides) blends ideas from both systems and is known for some of the
sharpest, most theoretically dense middlegame structures in modern practice.

## Grünfeld Defence (D70-D99)
1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.Nc3 d5 offers White a broad pawn centre in exchange for pressure
against it from a fianchettoed bishop on g7 and active piece play — a hypermodern
counterpart to the King's Indian that trades central space for dynamic counterattack.

## King's Indian Defence (E60-E99)
1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 fianchettoes the dark-squared bishop and allows White a full pawn
centre, planning a later central or kingside pawn break (...e5 or ...f5) to attack
it once development is complete. Known for sharp, opposite-wing attacking races,
particularly in the Classical and Sämisch main lines.

## Nimzo-Indian Defence (E20-E59)
1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4 pins the knight defending e4 immediately, often leading
White to accept doubled c-pawns in exchange for the bishop pair and central space —
a strategically rich opening prized for the clarity of its resulting structural
imbalances.

## Bogo-Indian and Queen's Indian Defences (E12-E19)
Played after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 (avoiding 3.Nc3, which would allow the
Nimzo-Indian pin), Black chooses between ...Bb4+ (Bogo-Indian) or a queenside
fianchetto with ...b6 (Queen's Indian) to contest the long diagonal and e4 without
committing to the sharper Nimzo structures.

## Catalan Opening (E00-E09)
Combining d4/c4 with a kingside fianchetto (g3 and Bg2), the Catalan pressures
Black's queenside and the long diagonal simultaneously, and is known for offering
White a small, persistent, low-risk structural edge that has made it a favourite at
the highest levels of classical chess.

## Dutch Defence (A80-A99)
1.d4 f5 stakes a claim on e4 immediately from the flank, at some cost to king safety
along the a2-g8 diagonal and the e-file. The Leningrad (with a kingside fianchetto)
and Stonewall (with a firm d5/e6/f5 pawn triangle) are its two most distinct
strategic setups.

## English Opening (A10-A39)
1.c4 approaches the centre from the flank and can transpose into Queen's Gambit,
Réti, or genuinely independent structures depending on both sides' follow-up. Its
flexibility is its main strategic asset: White can choose a symmetrical, Sicilian
Reversed, or four-knights-style setup depending on Black's response.

## Réti Opening (A04-A09)
1.Nf3, often followed by c4 and a kingside fianchetto, delays committing the centre
pawns at all, allowing White maximum flexibility to react to Black's setup —
frequently transposing into Catalan, English, or King's Indian Attack structures.

## London System (D02)
1.d4 followed by Bf4 (rather than developing normally and reacting to Black) gives
White a simple, repeatable, low-theory setup that is popular with club players and
increasingly seen at higher levels for its practical efficiency, aiming for a solid,
harmonious position without memorising deep opening theory.

## Colle System (D05)
1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 Nf6 3.e3 is another low-theory, solid system for White, keeping the
position flexible and aiming for a well-timed e3-e4 or c2-c4 central break once
development is complete rather than fighting for an opening-stage advantage directly.

## Bird's Opening (A02-A03)
1.f4 stakes an early claim on e5 from the flank, giving the game a Dutch-Defence-like
character with colours reversed, and is chosen more for its surprise value and
sidestepping of mainstream theory than for any inherent structural advantage.

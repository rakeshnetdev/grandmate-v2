## Weak King Safety
King safety is measured by how exposed the king is to checks and attacks: how many of
its pawn shield remain in place, how many files and diagonals lead directly to it, and
how many enemy pieces already bear on the squares around it. A king that has lost its
shield pawns or sits on a half-open file toward an enemy rook is structurally weak even
if no immediate tactic exists yet — the weakness is a standing liability the opponent
can build an attack around over several moves.

## Pawn Structure Damage
Doubled pawns (two pawns of the same colour on one file), isolated pawns (no friendly
pawn on either adjacent file), and backward pawns (unable to advance safely because a
neighbouring pawn cannot support it) are all long-term structural weaknesses. They
don't lose material immediately, but they create fixed targets and weak squares in
front of them that an opponent can occupy with pieces, and they persist for the rest
of the game unless traded off or advanced at real cost.

## Passed Pawn Creation
A passed pawn has no enemy pawn on its own file or on either adjacent file standing
between it and promotion, meaning no enemy pawn can ever block or capture it on its
way forward. Passed pawns grow more dangerous as pieces are traded off, since fewer
pieces remain to blockade or attack them, which is why creating one — even at the cost
of some material or structure elsewhere — is a standard long-term plan, especially
heading into an endgame.

## Piece Activity Imbalance
Material count alone does not describe a position: a side with fewer, more active
pieces (better mobility, more central influence, more available squares) often stands
better than a side with material parity but cramped, passive pieces. Activity is
usually measured by how many safe squares each piece can reach and how much central
influence it exerts, averaged and compared across the position over a span of moves
rather than judged from a single snapshot.

## Bad Bishop
A bishop is "bad" when its own side's pawns are fixed on the same colour square the
bishop moves on, blocking its diagonals and limiting it to a narrow range of activity —
sometimes to defending those very pawns rather than attacking anything. The opposite,
a "good" bishop, has its own pawns mostly on the other colour, leaving its diagonals
open. Bad bishops are a common long-term structural disadvantage, particularly in
endgames where there is little else to distract from the problem.

## Open File Control
A file with no pawns on it (open) or with only one side's pawn on it (half-open) is
valuable real estate for a rook or queen, since it offers an uncontested path toward
the enemy position, including potentially all the way to the back rank. Controlling an
open file — being the side whose rook or queen occupies or contests it first — is a
recurring middlegame goal, often achieved by trading off the pawn that made a file
closed.

## Centre Control
The four central squares (d4, d5, e4, e5) and the ranks/files running through them
are strategically valuable because pieces posted or influencing them typically reach
more of the board than pieces on the flanks. Occupying the centre with pawns, or
influencing it from a distance with pieces, restricts the opponent's piece mobility
and is one of the oldest and most consistently taught opening principles.

## Space Advantage
A space advantage means one side's pawns have advanced further, claiming more squares
and giving that side's pieces more room to manoeuvre, while cramping the opponent's
pieces behind their own less-advanced pawns. Space is double-edged: advanced pawns
claim territory but can also become targets or overextend, so a space advantage is
most valuable when it can be maintained and backed up by piece activity rather than
existing as pawns alone.

## Development Lag
Development refers to bringing minor pieces (knights and bishops) off their starting
squares and connecting rooks, ideally completed within the first ten to fifteen moves
alongside king safety (commonly via castling). A side that lags in development —
still has undeveloped pieces well into the middlegame — is vulnerable to being
overrun before its full army can even participate in the game, since only the
developed pieces are actually available to defend or counterattack.

## Time Trouble Collapse
Time trouble refers to a player having very little clock time left relative to the
moves still required, and a "collapse" is the measurable drop in move accuracy that
follows — positions that were being played well earlier in the game are handled
markedly worse once the clock becomes the dominant pressure. This is a practical,
clock-driven phenomenon rather than a purely positional one, and it is why time
management is treated as a skill in its own right, separate from calculation ability.

## Isolated Queen's Pawn (IQP)
An isolated pawn on the d-file with no friendly pawns on the c- or e-files is common
enough, and consequential enough, to be treated as its own strategic theme rather than
just an instance of general pawn-structure damage. The side with the IQP typically
gets open lines and active piece play in the middlegame as compensation, while the
side facing it usually aims to trade pieces and reach an endgame where the pawn becomes
a simple, static weakness with no compensating activity left to justify it.

## Minority Attack
A minority attack is advancing a smaller group of pawns (typically two) against a
larger enemy pawn group on the same wing, aiming to trade in a way that leaves the
opponent with an isolated or backward pawn on an open file — a structural weakness
created deliberately rather than one that simply arose from tactics. It is a
textbook middlegame plan in several queen's-pawn openings, most associated with
symmetrical pawn structures on the queenside.

## Opposition (King and Pawn Endgames)
Opposition is the situation in a king-and-pawn endgame where the two kings face each
other with one square between them and it is the opponent's move — the side not to
move typically holds the more favourable position, since the side to move must give
ground. Understanding and correctly counting opposition (including "distant
opposition" further away on the same file, rank, or diagonal) is foundational to
converting or holding elementary king-and-pawn endgames.

## Passed Pawn Technique
Beyond simply creating a passed pawn, converting one into a win requires supporting
its advance with the king or pieces, restraining the enemy king from reaching it in
time (the "square of the pawn" is a common quick check for this), and timing the push
so the pawn cannot be blockaded permanently. A passed pawn that cannot be
practically escorted forward is often only a long-term asset rather than an
immediate winning resource.
